# Nike design language

## When to reach for this reference
Use this reference when designing for brands in fitness, sports, athleisure, streetwear, or high-energy lifestyle. It fits products that need to convey confidence, momentum, and empowerment — from running apps and training platforms to sneaker drops, event microsites, campaigns, or athlete profiles. Reach for Nike's language when the brief calls for boldness without clutter, a single hero moment per view, and copy that drives action with short imperative verbs. This is the right reference when the audience is aspirational and performance-driven, and the brand needs to feel fast, modern, and globally iconic. Avoid it for calm, meditative, or delicately editorial contexts — energy here is always forward.

## Brand personality
Nike projects relentless forward motion. The personality sits at the intersection of elite athleticism and street credibility — it is confident bordering on defiant, competitive but never petty, disciplined yet emotionally charged. There is an underlying seriousness: greatness demands work, and the design reflects that by stripping away decoration. But the mood is never corporate. It is human, raw, and visceral. Authenticity is the currency; every visual choice must feel earned, not styled. The brand persona is part coach — pushing you past your limit — and part teammate, in the trenches with you.

Design voice traits: bold, direct, kinetic, unapologetic, aspirational, gritty, inclusive, iconic.

## Color philosophy
The core palette is deliberately severe: black, white, and one committed brand color in its most saturated, unapologetic form. Black anchors the experience — it is the default background for key moments, creating a void that amplifies everything placed within it. White commands equal real estate, delivering punch-in-the-chest contrast and breathable negative space. The single accent color (Volt green, University Red, Game Royal blue, Total Orange, or a campaign-specific hero hue) acts as an exclamation point — never diluted, never pastel, never competing with a second accent. Use the accent sparingly: one CTA, one highlight, one graphic element per view. Photographic skin tones and environmental hues provide the only additional color variance. Gradients are rare; when they appear, they are two-tone and sharp, not blended mist. Pure neutrality (black and white photography, mono UI chrome) lets product and athlete own the frame.

## Typography approach
Type is an athlete on the page. Headlines are set in bold, condensed, geometric sans-serifs — Futura Bold Condensed Extra Black, Trade Gothic Bold Condensed No. 20, or custom Futura-derived cuts — running edge-to-edge at massive scale: 80–200px on desktop, filling 60–80% of the viewport height on mobile. Display type is treated as graphic architecture, often cropped by the viewport edge or intersecting photography. Body copy inverts to minimalist sans-serif sets at restrained sizes (14–18px), creating maximum contrast between the shouted headline and the whispered detail. Letter spacing on headlines is tight or default-tight; body copy opens slightly for readability at small sizes. All caps is reserved for navigation labels, product nomenclature, and short imperative CTAs — never for body paragraphs. Type hierarchy is binary: giant hero headline or small functional text. There is no middle tier. Color is always black or white high-contrast against its background; tinted or mid-tone type is forbidden.

Primary fonts by tier:
- **Display/Headlines:** Futura Bold Condensed, Trade Gothic Bold Condensed, or system-condensed black. Scale: enormous, viewport-filling.
- **Navigation & CTAs:** Same condensed family at functional sizes (14–18px), frequently all-caps, frequently paired with a single-color underline or background strip.
- **Body:** Helvetica Neue, Inter, or system sans-serif; regular weight; 14–18px; generous line-height (1.6–1.75). Maximum 65 characters per line.

## Spacing & density
Space is never neutral — it is a weapon. Nike design uses extreme generosity: a single product, a single athlete, a single headline, then an ocean of negative space. Margins and padding are multiples of a 4px or 8px grid but scale dramatically: 80–200px outer gutters on desktop, collapsing proportionally but never below 20px on mobile. The rule is one focal point per screen. If the user can look at two things at once, the layout has failed. Stack is preferred over side-by-side; when horizontal layouts are unavoidable (product grids, metadata rows), items are spaced generously enough that each reads as a discrete moment. Section-to-section vertical rhythm is aggressive — 120px–240px gaps between content blocks — to force the eye through deliberate pauses. Density oscillates: heavy black section, then pure white breathing room, then heavy again. This binary rhythm is a signature.

## Corner radius & shape language
Sharp, squared, and architectural. Corner radius is effectively zero — 0px or 2px maximum — across cards, buttons, containers, and image masks. Rounded corners signal softness, and Nike has none. Product imagery may use a slight inner radius on the frame (0–2px) for technical polish, but the container itself is always rectangular. Shapes that do appear are bold and geometric: full-bleed rectangles, dramatic diagonal cuts, hard triangular callouts for promotional badges, and the Swoosh — which is itself a study in organic-but-precise curvature that contrasts the rigid geometry around it. The interplay between the organic Swoosh shape and the straight-line layout grid is a fundamental tension that makes the identity work. Do not introduce pill shapes, soft buttons, or bubbly decorative elements. When a shape is needed for a CTA or label, use a flat rectangle with the accent color as a filled background — no stroke, no radius, no gradient.

## Elevation & depth
Depth is predominantly flat. The visual language relies on contrast (black-and-white photography) and scale to create dimensionality, not shadow. When elevation is needed — such as on interactive cards or layered UI — it is expressed with minimal, hard-edged drop shadows (0px or 1px blur) or, more commonly, through solid color-plane stacking: a black rectangle slightly offset behind a white photograph creates more depth than any shadow. Z-height is communicated through opacity: a selected navigation item might sit on a solid black background strip while siblings sit on white. Do not use soft, diffuse, or multi-layered box shadows. Do not use glassmorphism, frosted overlays, or blurred backdrops. Overlays for text legibility are hard black rectangles at 40–60% opacity — never gradient fades, never blurs.

## Iconography & imagery
Photography is the primary visual medium. Nike does not illustrate — it photographs. Imagery is high-contrast, dynamic, and athlete-centered: mid-stride runners, airborne basketball players, sweat, motion blur, stadium light. Product photography follows the same rules: single hero shoe on black or white, aggressively lit from one side, hard shadows, no propping or lifestyle clutter unless the context is an on-foot street-style editorial. Photography fills 100% of the viewport or sits as a cutout on pure black/white. Full-bleed is the default.

Icons exist only where strictly necessary for UI — navigation hamburgers, cart, search, social platform marks. They are line-weight 1.5–2px, monochrome (black or white, never tinted), geometrically precise, and absolutely minimal. Icon grids are 24×24px. No filled icons, no duotone, no decorative iconography. The Swoosh is sometimes used as a functional UI element (progress indicator, loading spinner variant, scroll affordance) but never as decoration. When used functionally, it retains its proportions — do not stretch, distort, or outline the Swoosh.

Imagery DOs:
- High-contrast, directional lighting
- Athlete in motion, mid-action
- Shallow depth of field on product closeups
- Black and white with selective color accent
- Full-bleed or hard-edged cutout

Imagery DON'Ts:
- Lifestyle props (coffee cups, notebooks, plants)
- Flat-lay product photography
- Low-contrast or flat lighting
- Collage or scrapbook compositions

## Signature patterns
1. **Hero Singularity.** Exactly one hero element per viewport: one product, one athlete, one headline. Nothing competes.
2. **The Swoosh as Structural Anchor.** The Swoosh appears once per canvas — typically top-left or centered as a lockup with "JUST DO IT." or a category badge — and its position defines the grid for everything else.
3. **Black/White Binary Grid.** Layout alternates between full-black sections and full-white sections. Content on black uses white type and photography with dark backgrounds; content on white uses black type and cutout photography. This binary pulse creates rhythm across scroll.
4. **Justified Type Blocks.** Headlines are set justified-left but treated as architectural masses — frequently cropped, stacked, or interrupted by photography so that the text feels half wordmark, half object.
5. **Countdown / Launch Urgency.** For product drops, a bold countdown timer rendered in the hero condensed face with the accent color as a background bar creates ticking-clock tension. Limited palette: numerals only, no decorative framing.
6. **Vertical Split with Diagonal.** When two pieces of content must share a viewport (rare), use a hard diagonal edge (30–45 degrees) rather than a vertical split — the angle creates forward lean and visual momentum.
7. **Imperative CTAs.** Every call to action is a short imperative verb phrase on a contrasting background strip: "Shop," "Run," "Customize," "Join Us," "Get Notified." No soft language, no "Learn More," no punctuation.
8. **Athlete Story as Navigation.** Long-form landing pages use athlete photography as navigation cards — a full-bleed image of the athlete with their name in bold condensed type overlaid. Tapping enters their story.

## Motion philosophy
Motion is fast, decisive, and purposeful. Transitions are sharp (150–200ms ease-out), never languid or spring-bouncy. Page navigation uses hard cuts or rapid opacity fades. Product carousels snap — no momentum scrolling, no rubber-banding. The loading state is the Swoosh emerging from a single sweeping stroke animation (a continuous line drawn over ~600ms). Scroll-triggered reveals animate content in from the edges with short travel distance (40–60px) and fast duration (300–400ms). Parallax is used aggressively for hero photography: the athlete or product moves at 0.5× scroll speed while text overlays hold position, creating a sense of the subject pulling forward. Video autoplay without sound is common — short, looping athlete clips (3–8 seconds) that act as living photographs. Never use wobble, bounce, fade-to-scale, or decorative micro-interactions. The only acceptable easing beyond linear is a sharp ease-out curve. If a transition calls attention to itself rather than to the content, remove it.

## Voice & copy tone
Copy is short, imperative, and driven by hard consonants. Sentences average 3–5 words. Headlines are commands or provocations, not descriptions. The default mode is second-person direct address — "You" is always the athlete, even if the word is omitted. Punctuation is minimal: periods are rare in headlines, absent in CTAs, used only in body microcopy for legibility. Commas appear only when structurally necessary. Exclamation marks are forbidden — confidence does not need a crutch.

Headline examples:
- "You Can't Stop Us."
- "Dream Crazier."
- "Play New."
- "Don't Ask If It's Possible. Make It Possible."

CTA examples:
- "Shop"
- "Run"
- "Get Notified"
- "Customize"
- "Join Us"

Voice guardrails:
- **DO:** Short imperatives, direct address, verbs over adjectives, concrete over abstract, confident not arrogant.
- **DON'T:** Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions in body copy, passive voice ("The shoe was designed for..."), jargon, filler words ("really," "very," "actually"), soft CTAs ("Learn More," "Discover," "Explore").

## Explicitly do not
- Use rounded corners, pill buttons, soft shadows, frosted glass, or blurred overlays
- Introduce more than one accent color per screen or section
- Stack more than three type sizes on screen at once (hero headline, functional label, body microcopy)
- Use pastels, muted palettes, or light-mode-only designs without a black-mode counterpart
- Place multiple competing CTAs in the same viewport — there is one primary action, always
- Use lifestyle or flat-lay photography with props
- Add decorative illustration or iconography beyond the Swoosh and essential UI icons
- Use gradient fades, soft transitions, spring physics, or bounce animations
- Write body copy longer than two short paragraphs without a visual break
- Place the Swoosh more than once per canvas or distort its proportions
- Use exclamation marks
- Employ serif typefaces for any purpose — the Nike identity lives entirely in sans-serif geometry
- Add ornamental dividers, rules, borders, or decorative frames
- Use mid-tone gray type on any background — type is always black on white or white on black
- Allow two focal-point elements to occupy the same viewport at equal visual weight
